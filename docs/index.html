01 / THE QUESTION

Learn the tuning map.
Check the control behavior.

For a stable second-order plant with optional dead time, we want the output to track a unit step quickly, with no more than 10% overshoot. Numerical optimization supplies reference gains. A regressor learns to approximate those gains from measurable response features.

Our hypothesis: nonlinear models can learn this mapping better than a simple linear baseline, while predicting gains much faster than rerunning the optimizer.

A small gain error does not automatically mean a safe response.

We report gain accuracy and simulate each predicted controller. Stability, overshoot, convergence and gain bounds determine acceptance. ITAE and the regularized cost describe the tracking tradeoff among accepted controllers.

APPLICATIONS / THE PLANT FAMILY

Where could this kind of tuner help?

We study stable, positive-gain second-order plants with optional dead time: two poles, a constant numerator and a finite steady-state output. A measured step response supplies the five inputs; the model then proposes PID gains without requiring the physical parameters to be identified explicitly.

Positioning a damped mechanism

A mass attached to a spring and damper maps applied force to displacement. PID feedback can make displacement follow a requested position while controlling oscillation.

Regulating an electrical output

A series RLC circuit measured across its capacitor has this second-order form. The tuning task is to make its output voltage track a reference with limited overshoot.

Approximating a process response

Two dominant stable lags can approximate some thermal or process-control dynamics, sometimes with sensor or transport delay. A learned tuner could provide an initial controller when that approximation is appropriate.

These are motivating applications, not hardware experiments. Our evidence covers the stated simulation ranges and ideal PID only.

01 / WHAT IS A SYSTEM?

A relationship between input and output.

A physical system, or plant, responds to an input over time. A motor turns voltage into motion; an RLC circuit turns applied voltage into capacitor voltage. Its dynamics determine how quickly the output moves, whether it oscillates, and where it settles.

02 / THE TRANSFER FUNCTION

A compact model of the dynamics.

For a linear, time-invariant system starting from rest, G(s) = Y(s) / U(s) relates input and output in the Laplace domain. The model below describes a stable second-order plant with gain K, natural frequency ωn, damping ratio ζ, and an optional delay L. These parameters determine its response to an input.

SECOND-ORDER PLANT + DELAYG(s) = Kωn2s2 + 2ζωns + ωn2 e−Ls

KSets the eventual output for a unit input.

ωnSets a characteristic speed of response.

ζShapes damping and oscillation.

LDelays when the response begins.

03 / OPEN LOOP

Let the plant respond on its own.

A unit step changes the input from 0 to 1. Without feedback, the input stays at 1 regardless of what the output does. For this plant family, the output eventually approaches K; it does not automatically track a desired output of 1.

SAME PLANT / ADJUSTABLE CONTROLLER

Choose a desired output, the setpoint r(t). Negative feedback compares it with the actual output: e(t) = r(t) − y(t). The ideal PID controller C(s) = Kp + Ki/s + Kd·s uses three actions to turn that error into a changing plant input. Together, the three terms determine the input applied through a single feedback loop.

P / PRESENT

Proportional

Acts on the error now. A larger Kp reacts more strongly, but can increase oscillation.

Kp × e(t)
I / PAST

Integral

Accumulates error over time. It can remove persistent offset in a stable loop, but too much can cause overshoot.

Ki × ∫ e(t) dt
D / RATE OF CHANGE

Derivative

Acts on how fast the error changes. It can add damping; ideal differentiation is sensitive to noise and produces a reference-step impulse.

Kd × de(t)/dt

THE DATA / SOURCE & SCOPE

Synthetic plants. Independently checked labels.

No ML model generates the training labels: SLSQP is a numerical optimization algorithm. There is no external measured dataset. Python generates stable SOPDT plants with independent uniform draws: K ∈ [0.5, 2], ωn ∈ [0.5, 10] rad/s, ζ ∈ [0.1, 2]. Dead time τ is exactly zero with 10% probability; otherwise it is uniform on (0, 1] s. Continuous batch draws are not rounded to the browser controls’ 0.01 resolution.

Failed tuning attempts are logged and replaced until the requested number of accepted rows is reached. This makes the dataset useful for supervised learning, but introduces selection bias toward plants our teacher can label. A separate rejected-plant experiment examines that boundary.

How are the five measured features defined?

The open-loop recording starts at 10 s and doubles until its tail settles, up to 640 s, at 0.01 s sampling. In the final 20%, the output range must be within 0.2% of the absolute tail mean, and the means of its two halves must agree within 0.1%. The final sample estimates yss.

InputMeasurement ruleWhy include it?
triseFirst 10% to first 90% of estimated final outputResponse speed
tsettlingLast sample outside the ±2% final-output bandDecay / damping information
ypeakMaximum recorded outputOscillation / amplification
yssLast output after the tail checkMeasured steady-state gain
τestFirst crossing of 1% of final outputOnset proxy, not exact physical delay

Only these five features enter the models. K, ωn, ζ and τ are evaluation metadata, not privileged prediction inputs. Feature extraction was rechecked on 32 stratified spot-check plants; maximum absolute discrepancy was 4.44 × 10⁻¹⁶.

Why regularize ITAE, and how trustworthy is a label?

With ideal PID, especially at zero delay, minimizing ITAE alone can reward increasingly large gains. We therefore minimize a speed-normalized tracking cost plus a dimensionless gain penalty:

ITAE = ∫0∞ t |1 − y(t)| dt
J = ωn2 ITAE + λRλ = 0.01
R = (K Kp)2+ (K Ki / ωn)2+ (K ωn Kd)2
The integral uses a converged finite simulation window. The dimensionless penalty discourages large gains; it does not measure actuator energy. The 10% overshoot constraint is checked separately.

A matched 200-plant calibration compared penalty strengths. Both 0.001 and 0.01 produced 196 accepted labels; 0.001 still hit an upper gain bound once, while 0.01 did not. We chose 0.01 as a tracking-versus-gain tradeoff, not as a proof that every future gain stays away from its bounds.

Up to four feasible starting controllers are selected from heuristic backoffs and conservative PI probes, screened for stable poles and sampled overshoot ≤9.5%. SLSQP minimizes J subject to the 10% overshoot constraint. Bounds are Kp, Ki ∈ [0.001, 100] and Kd ∈ [0, 50]. We keep the lowest-J independently accepted result. These are best-found local reference gains, not certified global optima.

A converged optimizer is not enough: separate checks require finite bounded gains, stable poles (largest real part < −10⁻⁸), refined overshoot ≤10% within a 10⁻⁶ percentage-point tolerance, tail error ≤0.001, and converged metrics. ITAE refinement tolerance is 0.1%; peak refinement tolerance is 0.001 percentage points. Horizons can extend to 1,000 s and the standard grid cap is 65,537 points. Hitting a resource cap is unresolved, not proof of instability.

QUALITY / WHY SOME ROWS ARE REJECTED

A plant needs a trustworthy label.

We attempted 10,083 plants to obtain exactly 10,000 accepted rows. A plant is rejected only when no candidate search returns an independently accepted controller. The sampled open-loop plant is stable; the candidate closed-loop controller may not be.

Rejected attempts are logged and replaced by new plants. This introduces selection bias toward plants our labeling pipeline can handle. Rejection is not proof that PID control is impossible. In Results & Evidence, we test the frozen models on the 83 rejected plants, distinguishing strict validation from an expanded numerical grid budget.

A MODELING CHOICE / WHY PADÉ?

A practical approximation.
An explicit limit.

The physical plant contains a true time shift. During numerical tuning we use a second-order Padé approximation, which replaces the delay with a rational transfer function. This makes the closed loop compatible with ordinary polynomial pole checks and standard LTI simulations during repeated candidate evaluations.

This is a computational modeling choice, not a requirement of PID control. True-delay optimization is possible. We did not benchmark an end-to-end true-delay optimizer, so we do not claim a measured generation speedup over one.

e−τs ≈ P2(s) / Q2(s)
Open-loop feature extraction retains the analytical true delay. Label optimization and the primary closed-loop evaluation use Padé; delays at or below 10−5 s are treated as zero in that evaluator.

The approximation matters.

Our labels and main scores describe the Padé model. An independent true-delay stress test reduced MLP passes from 97 to 84 on the same 100 plants. Thirteen previously accepted controllers crossed the overshoot limit.

The stress evidence below shows these discrepancies, including a case changing from 8.94% to 33.23% overshoot. Padé validation is not an exact-delay or hardware guarantee.

03 / TUNE

Find gains that meet the objective.

CONSTRAINED NUMERICAL OPTIMIZATION
BALANCE TRACKING AND GAINS

ITAE = ∫0T t |1 − y(t)| dt

ITAE means Integral of Time-weighted Absolute Error. Multiplying error by time penalizes errors that persist. The desired output is 1; T is the simulation duration.
SUBJECT TO

Overshoot ≤ 10%

For a target of 1, the output peak must stay within 1.10.
Kp, Ki ∈ [0.001, 100] · Kd ∈ [0, 50]

The tuning objective is J = ωn2 ITAE + 0.01 [(K Kp)2 + (K Ki / ωn)2 + (K ωn Kd)2]. The penalty discourages aggressive gains; it is not actuator energy and does not guarantee that bounds are never reached. The table reports raw ITAE for interpretation; selection uses J.

Tuning means searching for values of Kp, Ki, and Kd that give a good closed-loop response. The search needs an initial set of gains. Because different starting gains can lead to different results, this method runs up to four separate searches.

  1. Build trial controllers

    A formula based on the plant parameters supplies an initial estimate. Smaller versions of those gains and controllers with Kd = 0 provide additional candidates.

  2. Check the starting gains

    A candidate can start a search only if its closed-loop model is stable and its measured overshoot is at most 9.5%. This leaves a small margin below the final 10% limit. Up to four distinct candidates are chosen.

  3. Improve and compare

    SLSQP, a numerical optimizer, adjusts each candidate’s gains to reduce normalized ITAE plus a squared-gain penalty while enforcing the constraints. Final controllers are checked again, and the accepted result with the lowest combined objective is kept.

These are local numerical searches: a better solution may exist elsewhere. The gains in the table are the starting values; “Accepted” describes the controller obtained after optimization and validation. This stage generates training targets without using a trained machine-learning model.

RESEARCH / LEARNING TO TUNE

03 / THE METHOD

Five measurements.
Six ways to learn.

A linear model sets the floor. Polynomial regression, tree ensembles and a small neural network test whether nonlinear structure helps. Every model uses the same empirical inputs.

01 / PREPROCESSING

Put the targets on a comparable scale.

Fast plants and slow plants need gains on different scales. Instead of predicting raw gains, we train all models on targets normalized using the measured final output and rise time. This uses no true physical parameters.

qp = yss Kp
qi = yss trise Ki
qd = yss Kdtrise

Decode the prediction before evaluating the controller:

Kp = qpyssKi = qiyss triseKd = qd triseyss

Fit preprocessing on training data only.

Linear, ridge, polynomial and neural inputs are standardized with training statistics. Tree models use the five inputs without standardization. Polynomial regression expands them to 20 degree-1 and degree-2 terms.

The MLP applies a standardized log-space loss to positive normalized targets. This reduces domination by large gain magnitudes. Its scales and positive-output calibration are fitted only on training data.

There is no inference-time clipping, fallback controller or access to the true K, ωn, ζ, τ. The main comparison assumes complete, noise-free empirical inputs. Separate stress tests measure how this assumption breaks down under noise; missing empirical features remain unsupported.

The network learns by repeatedly adjusting its weights to reduce prediction error. We keep the checkpoint with the lowest validation loss, rather than simply using the last training step.

Training algorithm and reproducible settings

AdamW is an adaptive gradient-based training algorithm with weight decay, which discourages unnecessarily large network weights. This trains the MLP; it is separate from the numerical PID optimizer used to create labels. Learning rate: 0.001; weight decay: 0.0001; batch size: 256. Training allows 500 epochs with early-stopping patience 50. The final run used that budget and restored its best validation-loss checkpoint. Positive softplus outputs are scaled by training-target medians.

The loss, and the physics-informed alternative

The deployed study model minimizes mean squared error in log(q + 0.001), standardized by training-target log standard deviations. Neural weight decay and gradient clipping regularize training; they do not impose the closed-loop overshoot constraint.

A physics-informed Hurwitz stability penalty was explored separately on an earlier 1,000-row experiment. Across three seeds it did not consistently improve acceptance (80.04% versus 80.46% for the standard MLP). The final 10K study therefore uses the standard MLP with physics-loss weight zero. This historical experiment is not a final-cohort ablation.

03 / THE EVALUATION PROTOCOL

Train. Select. Then test once.

7,000 TRAIN1,500 VAL1,500 TEST

Plant rows are split once, stratified by exactly-zero versus positive delay (fixed seeds 42 / 43). All model variants share this partition. Preprocessing is fitted on the 7,000 training plants.

Candidate settings are selected primarily by validation closed-loop acceptance, then by J among accepted controllers. The validation partition also controls MLP early stopping. Settings are locked before the 1,500 held-out test plants are scored.

Learning curves use nested training subsets and one fixed 500-plant stratified validation subset. They are not repeated tests on the holdout, and are single-run trends rather than uncertainty estimates.

Two levels of evaluation.

Gain regression: per-gain MAE, RMSE and R2 against best-found numerical labels. The overview R2 is the uniform average across Kp, Ki and Kd.

Controller behavior: decode the gains, rebuild the unity-feedback loop and independently check bounds, poles, refined overshoot, steady-state tail and numerical convergence.

Tracking quality: ITAE ratio = predicted / reference; J ratio = predicted / reference. A ratio of 1 matches the numerical teacher. Ratios are reported only where both controllers pass, with the coverage shown.

What “accepted” means. It means the full numerical validation passed for the simulated plant and modeling assumptions. It does not guarantee a fixed ITAE degradation, physical actuator feasibility, or real-world safety.

05 / DATASET SIZE

More data helps.
Choose a practical training budget.

Our final 10,000-row dataset supplies 7,000 training examples. The decision balances model performance, numerical labeling cost, reproducibility and a manageable undergraduate study. Larger datasets can still improve acceptance; 10K is not a proven optimum or a deployment safety threshold.

How is this curve a fair comparison?

Every point uses the same 500 final-validation plants and fixed model settings. Training prefixes are nested: the original final-training rows, then independent plants from the earlier development cohort. MLP stopping uses the same full validation partition. The final test set is never used or rescored for these curves. Extended fits are development diagnostics and do not replace the frozen 7K-trained model used in the live explorer. The axis counts training plants, not total dataset rows: the final 10K dataset corresponds to the 7K marker. Settings and early stopping use validation data, so these curves can be optimistic. The held-out table is the independent final assessment. This single-seed diagnostic is not a confidence interval.

How were metrics completed for overshoot misses?

A separate evaluator continues after an overshoot violation. It retains the same pole criterion, gain-bound reporting, grid/horizon limits, ITAE and peak convergence tolerances, and tail check. It measures 2% settling time and converged ITAE without changing any strict flags. All 1,500 controllers per nonlinear model resolved; some stable linear-model responses reached resource limits and remain unresolved. These diagnostics concern the Padé plant, not a real-system safety certificate.

07 / ROBUSTNESS STRESS TESTS

Test the assumptions.
Show where they break.

The main test uses clean measurements and the same simulated plant model used for training. A stress test changes one of those assumptions to see whether a controller still works. We test noisy measurements, changes in the physical plant, an exact delay instead of its approximation, plants near our parameter limits, and an unexpected external load.

We compare the already-trained MLP, Random Forest and XGBoost with numerical-reference controllers. No model learns from these tests. Four experiments reuse the same 100 test plants, including 10 with zero delay; the parameter-limit test adds 241 fresh plants. Repeated trials produce 14,964 controller outcomes, not that many independent plants. Unless stated otherwise, a pass requires all original checks, including at most 10% overshoot. The load-disturbance test uses a separate recovery criterion.

01 · Measurement noise

The question. Do inaccurate sensor readings lead the model to choose poor PID gains?

What we did. We add random Gaussian noise to each plant’s open-loop response, extract the same five features again, and predict new gains. Noise ranges from 0.01% to 2% of plant gain K, with three noisy recordings per plant at each level. We then test those gains on the original plant. For the plotted comparison, we supply the clean recording duration so that failure to stop recording does not hide the effect on feature extraction.

Why trust this independent simulator?

A method-of-steps solver stores delayed state history and includes the ideal derivative impulse as the correct velocity jump at the physical delay. Two solver tolerances, two output grids, refined peaks and extended horizons must agree. Limits are 1,000 s, 6,000 delay intervals and 65,537 output points. What we did. We generate 25 fresh plants in each of nine targeted groups, such as low damping, high gain or very small delay, plus all 16 minimum/maximum parameter combinations. 05 · Load disturbance recovery

The question. Can the controller restore the output after an unexpected external load, even though the setpoint has not changed?

What we did. We start each of the 100 plants at the settled unit setpoint with its controller already operating. We then add a permanent input disturbance of 0.1/K, with the plant’s delay included. Without corrective feedback, this would shift the final output by 0.1. We keep the original gains and use the Padé plant model.

How to read the result. The table reports how many responses recover, their largest output deviation, recovery time and disturbance ITAE. Recovery means returning within ±0.01 of the setpoint, with converged tail error at most 0.001. Lower deviation, time and ITAE are better. This is a disturbance-rejection test, so the reference-step 10% overshoot rule is not its pass criterion.

All 100 controllers per family recover under the tested Padé model. This evaluates the plant-input disturbance path G/(1 + CG). The setpoint stays fixed, so there is no derivative impulse from a setpoint change.

Scope, sampling uncertainty and reproducibility

The same controller/plant cases are paired across models. Noise has 300 recordings per level; uncertainty has 500 perturbations per level, clustered around 100 plants. CSVs include plant-cluster bootstrap intervals. A numerical reference using true physical parameters is an oracle comparison for noisy-feature inference; it does not have to infer its gains from corrupted features. These post-test stress experiments do not provide a new independent main accuracy estimate. They retain raw gains, failure reasons and numerical limits. No hardware, actuator saturation, filtered derivative or nonlinear plant was tested.

09 / WHY USE ML?

Amortize the search.
Keep the verification.

The edge is repeated, cheap inference.

A numerical optimizer needs a plant model and repeatedly simulates candidate gains. After offline training, our MLP predicts from five empirical features in one forward pass, avoiding explicit physical-parameter identification at inference. The labeling/search cost is paid upfront and amortized over many future predictions.

This is a useful shortcut within the learned family. Numerical optimization remains useful when a plant model is known, requirements change or a new reference controller is needed.

Speed is not a safety certificate.

ML occasionally misses the overshoot limit. A step response still has to be recorded; feature collection can dominate inference time. Independent verification adds cost and is harder on an unknown real plant than on a known synthetic model.

05 / CONCLUSION & REPRODUCTION

A useful shortcut.
A measured conclusion.

MLP is our preferred overall model: compact, fast and strongest on the main test. The stress evidence also identifies important conditions where the current pipeline needs improvement.

WHAT WE LEARNED

Fast inference. Conditional performance.

Five noise-free empirical features can approximate regularized numerical PID tuning within our stable, positive-gain SOPDT family. The MLP meets all strict Padé checks on 93.73% of the held-out plants, with median accepted-pair ITAE 1.058× the numerical reference. All 1,500 MLP responses are pole-stable and numerically resolved; 97.8% stay within 12% overshoot. A small specification miss does not make a controller meaningless.

The model predicts in about 0.144 ms in our warm CPU benchmark, versus 1.391 s for numerical tuning plus validation. Adding independent verification makes the workflow about 11.6× faster on jointly accepted timing cases. Feature collection, loading and offline labeling/training are separate costs.

These findings support a fast candidate tuner within the studied family. They do not establish a universal, hardware-ready, missing-feature-tolerant or safety-certified controller.

Padé defines the main result.

True-delay stress passes were 84/100 for MLP versus 97/100 under Padé on the same cohort. The teacher also loses passes with true delay. Approximation error must be addressed before claiming equivalent exact-delay performance.

Measurements need better handling.

The original tail rule is noise-sensitive; every tested recording at ≥0.05% noise failed it. Given a supplied recording window, learned controllers also deteriorate at larger noise levels. Missing empirical features are unsupported. Physical-input mode derives the same five features; physical parameters are not extra network inputs.

Some boundaries favor a baseline.

At high normalized delay, Random Forest passed 22/25 boundary plants and MLP 11/25. Tiny positive delays often exhausted the Padé numerical grid budget. A resolution limit is neither a validated response nor proof of instability.

The teacher and dataset introduce bias.

Four-start SLSQP finds local solutions under chosen gain bounds and λ = 0.01. We replace unlabelled plants, so the main population favors plants the teacher can handle. Rejected-plant recovery is 21/83 at the original MLP grid budget and 64/83 after resolution-only retries; these are different numerical budgets.

One cohort is not every deployment.

Main scores use one split and training seed; the extended learning curves are validation-guided development evidence. Stress trials repeat 100 plants, not thousands of independent plants. Stable synthetic plants, ideal differentiation, a unit setpoint and linear dynamics do not cover saturation, nonlinearities, hardware, filtered derivatives or arbitrary disturbances.
