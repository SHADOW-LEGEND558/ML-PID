01 / THE QUESTION

Learn the tuning map.
Check the control behavior.

For a stable second-order plant with optional dead time, we want the output to track a unit step quickly, with no more than 10% overshoot. Numerical optimization supplies reference gains. A regressor learns to approximate those gains from measurable response features.

Our hypothesis: nonlinear models can learn this mapping better than a simple linear baseline, while predicting gains much faster than rerunning the optimizer.

A small gain error does not automatically mean a safe response.

We report gain accuracy and simulate each predicted controller. Stability, overshoot, convergence and gain bounds determine acceptance. ITAE and the regularized cost describe the tracking tradeoff among accepted controllers.

01 / WHAT IS A SYSTEM?

A relationship between input and output.

A physical system, or plant, responds to an input over time. A motor turns voltage into motion; an RLC circuit turns applied voltage into capacitor voltage. Its dynamics determine how quickly the output moves, whether it oscillates, and where it settles.

02 / THE TRANSFER FUNCTION

A compact model of the dynamics.

For a linear, time-invariant system starting from rest, G(s) = Y(s) / U(s) relates input and output in the Laplace domain. The model below describes a stable second-order plant with gain K, natural frequency ωn, damping ratio ζ, and an optional delay L. These parameters determine its response to an input.

SECOND-ORDER PLANT + DELAYG(s) = Kωn2s2 + 2ζωns + ωn2 e−Ls

KSets the eventual output for a unit input.

ωnSets a characteristic speed of response.

ζShapes damping and oscillation.

LDelays when the response begins.

03 / OPEN LOOP

Let the plant respond on its own.

A unit step changes the input from 0 to 1. Without feedback, the input stays at 1 regardless of what the output does. For this plant family, the output eventually approaches K; it does not automatically track a desired output of 1.

04 / CLOSE THE LOOP

Measure. Compare. Correct.

SAME PLANT / ADJUSTABLE CONTROLLER

Choose a desired output, the setpoint r(t). Negative feedback compares it with the actual output: e(t) = r(t) − y(t). The ideal PID controller C(s) = Kp + Ki/s + Kd·s uses three actions to turn that error into a changing plant input. Together, the three terms determine the input applied through a single feedback loop.

P / PRESENT

Proportional

Acts on the error now. A larger Kp reacts more strongly, but can increase oscillation.

Kp × e(t)
I / PAST

Integral

Accumulates error over time. It can remove persistent offset in a stable loop, but too much can cause overshoot.

Ki × ∫ e(t) dt
D / RATE OF CHANGE

Derivative

Acts on how fast the error changes. It can add damping; ideal differentiation is sensitive to noise and produces a reference-step impulse.

Kd × de(t)/dt

THE DATA / SOURCE & SCOPE

Synthetic plants. Independently checked labels.

There is no external measured dataset. Python generates stable SOPDT plants with independent uniform draws: K ∈ [0.5, 2], ωn ∈ [0.5, 10] rad/s, ζ ∈ [0.1, 2]. Dead time τ is exactly zero with 10% probability; otherwise it is uniform on (0, 1] s. Continuous batch draws are not rounded to the browser controls’ 0.01 resolution.

Failed tuning attempts are logged and replaced until the requested number of accepted rows is reached. This makes the dataset useful for supervised learning, but introduces selection bias toward plants our teacher can label. A separate rejected-plant experiment examines that boundary.

How are the five measured features defined?

The open-loop recording starts at 10 s and doubles until its tail settles, up to 640 s, at 0.01 s sampling. In the final 20%, the output range must be within 0.2% of the absolute tail mean, and the means of its two halves must agree within 0.1%. The final sample estimates yss.

InputMeasurement ruleWhy include it?
triseFirst 10% to first 90% of estimated final outputResponse speed
tsettlingLast sample outside the ±2% final-output bandDecay / damping information

yssLast output after the tail checkMeasured steady-state gain
τestFirst crossing of 1% of final outputOnset proxy, not exact physical delay

Only these five features enter the models. AUC was removed to keep the input definition simple. K, ωn, ζ and τ are evaluation metadata, not privileged prediction inputs. Feature extraction was rechecked on 32 stratified spot-check plants; maximum absolute discrepancy was 4.44 × 10⁻¹⁶.

Why regularize ITAE, and how trustworthy is a label?

With ideal PID, especially at zero delay, minimizing ITAE alone can reward increasingly large gains. We therefore minimize a speed-normalized tracking cost plus a dimensionless gain penalty:

ITAE = ∫ t |1 − y(t)| dt
J = ωn2 ITAE + 0.01 [(K Kp)2 + (K Ki / ωn)2 + (K ωn Kd)2]The integral is evaluated over a converged finite simulation window. The gain penalty discourages aggressive controllers; it is not a measure of actuator energy.

A matched 200-plant calibration compared penalty strengths. Both 0.001 and 0.01 produced 196 accepted labels; 0.001 still hit an upper gain bound once, while 0.01 did not. We chose 0.01 as a tracking-versus-gain tradeoff, not as a proof that every future gain stays away from its bounds.

Up to four feasible starting controllers are selected from heuristic backoffs and conservative PI probes, screened for stable poles and sampled overshoot ≤9.5%. SLSQP minimizes J subject to the 10% overshoot constraint. Bounds are Kp, Ki ∈ [0.001, 100] and Kd ∈ [0, 50]. We keep the lowest-J independently accepted result. These are best-found local reference gains, not certified global optima.

A converged optimizer is not enough: separate checks require finite bounded gains, stable poles (largest real part < −10⁻⁸), refined overshoot ≤10% within a 10⁻⁶ percentage-point tolerance, tail error ≤0.001, and converged metrics. ITAE refinement tolerance is 0.1%; peak refinement tolerance is 0.001 percentage points. Horizons can extend to 1,000 s and the standard grid cap is 65,537 points. Hitting a resource cap is unresolved, not proof of instability.

QUALITY / WHY SOME ROWS ARE REJECTED

A plant needs a trustworthy label.

We attempted 10,083 plants to obtain exactly 10,000 accepted rows. A plant is rejected only when no candidate search returns an independently accepted controller. The sampled open-loop plant is stable; the candidate closed-loop controller may not be.

03 / TUNE

Find gains that meet the objective.

CONSTRAINED NUMERICAL OPTIMIZATION
BALANCE TRACKING AND GAINS

ITAE = ∫0T t |1 − y(t)| dt

ITAE means Integral of Time-weighted Absolute Error. Multiplying error by time penalizes errors that persist. The desired output is 1; T is the simulation duration.
SUBJECT TO

Overshoot ≤ 10%

For a target of 1, the output peak must stay within 1.10.
Kp, Ki ∈ [0.001, 100] · Kd ∈ [0, 50]

The tuning objective is J = ωn2 ITAE + 0.01 [(K Kp)2 + (K Ki / ωn)2 + (K ωn Kd)2]. The penalty discourages aggressive gains; it is not actuator energy and does not guarantee that bounds are never reached. The table reports raw ITAE for interpretation; selection uses J.

Tuning means searching for values of Kp, Ki, and Kd that give a good closed-loop response. The search needs an initial set of gains. Because different starting gains can lead to different results, this method runs up to four separate searches.

  1. Build trial controllers

    A formula based on the plant parameters supplies an initial estimate. Smaller versions of those gains and controllers with Kd = 0 provide additional candidates.

  2. Check the starting gains

    A candidate can start a search only if its closed-loop model is stable and its measured overshoot is at most 9.5%. This leaves a small margin below the final 10% limit. Up to four distinct candidates are chosen.

  3. Improve and compare

    SLSQP, a numerical optimizer, adjusts each candidate’s gains to reduce normalized ITAE plus a squared-gain penalty while enforcing the constraints. Final controllers are checked again, and the accepted result with the lowest combined objective is kept.

These are local numerical searches: a better solution may exist elsewhere. The gains in the table are the starting values; “Accepted” describes the controller obtained after optimization and validation. This stage generates training targets without using a trained machine-learning model.

RESEARCH / LEARNING TO TUNE

03 / THE METHOD

Five measurements.
Six ways to learn.

A linear model sets the floor. Polynomial regression, tree ensembles and a small neural network test whether nonlinear structure helps. Every model uses the same empirical inputs.

01 / PREPROCESSING

Put the targets on a comparable scale.

Fast plants and slow plants need gains on different scales. Instead of predicting raw gains, we train all models on targets normalized using the measured final output and rise time. This uses no true physical parameters.

qp = yss Kp
qi = yss trise Ki
qd = yss Kdtrise

Decode the prediction before evaluating the controller:

Kp = qpyssKi = qiyss triseKd = qd triseyss

Fit preprocessing on training data only.

Linear, ridge, polynomial and neural inputs are standardized with training statistics. Tree models use the five inputs without standardization. Polynomial regression expands them to 20 degree-1 and degree-2 terms.

The MLP applies a standardized log-space loss to positive normalized targets. This reduces domination by large gain magnitudes. Its scales and positive-output calibration are fitted only on training data.

There is no inference-time clipping, fallback controller or access to the true K, ωn, ζ, τ. Missing or noisy empirical inputs are outside the evaluated setup.

The loss, and the physics-informed alternative

The deployed study model minimizes mean squared error in log(q + 0.001), standardized by training-target log standard deviations. Neural weight decay and gradient clipping regularize training; they do not impose the closed-loop overshoot constraint.

A physics-informed Hurwitz stability penalty was explored separately on an earlier 1,000-row experiment. Across three seeds it did not consistently improve acceptance (80.04% versus 80.46% for the standard MLP). Therefore the 20K study uses the standard MLP, with physics-loss weight zero. We do not claim these historical numbers are a 20K ablation.

03 / THE EVALUATION PROTOCOL

Train. Select. Then test once.

7,000 TRAIN1,500 VAL1,500 TEST

Plant rows are split once, stratified by exactly-zero versus positive delay (fixed seeds 42 / 43). All model variants share this partition. Preprocessing is fitted on the 7,000 training plants.

Candidate settings are selected primarily by validation closed-loop acceptance, then by J among accepted controllers. The validation partition also controls MLP early stopping. Settings are locked before the 1,500 held-out test plants are scored.

Learning curves use nested training subsets and one fixed 500-plant stratified validation subset. They are not repeated tests on the holdout, and are single-run trends rather than uncertainty estimates.

Two levels of evaluation.

Gain regression: per-gain MAE, RMSE and R2 against best-found numerical labels. The overview R2 is the uniform average across Kp, Ki and Kd.

Controller behavior: decode the gains, rebuild the unity-feedback loop and independently check bounds, poles, refined overshoot, steady-state tail and numerical convergence.

Tracking quality: ITAE ratio = predicted / reference; J ratio = predicted / reference. A ratio of 1 matches the numerical teacher. Ratios are reported only where both controllers pass, with the coverage shown.

What “accepted” means. It means the full numerical validation passed for the simulated plant and modeling assumptions. It does not guarantee a fixed ITAE degradation, physical actuator feasibility, or real-world safety.

05 / DATASET SIZE

More data helps.
Choose a practical training budget.

Our final 10,000-row dataset supplies 7,000 training examples. The decision balances model performance, numerical labeling cost, reproducibility and a manageable undergraduate study. Larger datasets can still improve acceptance; 10K is not a proven optimum or a deployment safety threshold.

06 / ERROR ANALYSIS

Investigate the failures, not just the average.

Ninety-four MLP controllers exceed the overshoot limit.

The MLP is pole-stable on all 1,500 main-test plants, yet only 1,406 pass the full acceptance criteria. All 94 failures exceed 10% overshoot: 61 fall between 10–12%, 28 between 12–15%, 3 between 15–20%, and 2 exceed 20%. Maximum measured overshoot is 27.53%. The controls above separate overshoot severity from missing numerical results. The case explorer includes an MLP failure and a random-forest failure, so strong averages do not hide individual misses.

Among accepted MLP controllers, the 90th-percentile ITAE ratio is 1.600× and the maximum is 9.61×; 78 of the 1,406 accepted pairs exceed 2×. Acceptance does not bound tracking regret. The “Accepted, but high ITAE” example makes this limitation visible.

Near-zero positive delays can create fast Padé modes that demand very fine simulation grids. A resolution-limit outcome is an unresolved numerical check; it must not be counted as either a proven unstable plant or a successful controller.

Some plant regions are harder.

The final MLP passes all 154 zero-delay test plants. Acceptance is 91.07% for dead time above 0.5 s and 91.48% for damping below 0.5. These descriptive patterns were not used for post-test tuning.

The teacher supplies local reference gains. Small gain errors can move a controller across the overshoot boundary even when aggregate gain R2 is high.

07 / REJECTED-PLANT STRESS TEST

A missing label is not an impossible plant.

We recovered the 83 plants rejected during final-data generation. Frozen final models predict gains from measured features without optimization or retraining. This deliberately difficult stress set is separate from the 1,500-plant test set.

The strict cap is 65,537 points. Only resolution-limit outcomes are retried with up to 262,145 points; gains, tolerances, overshoot limit and horizon cap do not change. Expanded-budget results are not standard-budget passes. There are no accepted teacher labels for these plants, so target-gain error and reference-relative ITAE cannot be computed.

08 / WHY USE ML?

Amortize the search.
Keep the verification.

The edge is repeated, cheap inference.

A numerical optimizer needs a plant model and repeatedly simulates candidate gains. After offline training, our MLP predicts from five empirical features in one forward pass, avoiding explicit physical-parameter identification at inference. The labeling/search cost is paid upfront and amortized over many future predictions.

This is a useful shortcut within the learned family. Numerical optimization remains useful when a plant model is known, requirements change or a new reference controller is needed.

Speed is not a safety certificate.

ML occasionally misses the overshoot limit. A step response still has to be recorded; feature collection can dominate inference time. Independent verification adds cost and is harder on an unknown real plant than on a known synthetic model.

Warm CPU timings on 20 validation plants; prediction excludes feature collection, loading and training. Verified-workflow speedups use jointly accepted cases. Timing is machine-specific and is not a new accuracy estimate.

RESEARCH / LIMITS & NEXT STEPS

05 / WHAT THE EVIDENCE SUPPORTS

A useful tuning shortcut.
A bounded conclusion.

Within this simulated family, nonlinear regression approximates numerical tuning well and predicts gains quickly. The model has not become a universal or safety-certified controller.

Simulation is the boundary of the result.

Plants are stable, linear, positive-gain SOPDT systems within fixed sampling ranges. We have not tested unstable plants, nonlinearities, disturbances, sensor noise, saturation or parameter drift. Ideal derivative action has a setpoint-step impulse and amplifies measurement noise. A real deployment needs a realizable derivative filter and actuator-aware retuning.

Delay modeling is approximate.

The analytical open-loop response includes true dead time, but optimization and closed-loop evaluation use a second-order Padé approximation (delays ≤10⁻⁵ s are treated as zero). Passing those checks is not an exact-delay robustness guarantee. An independent exact-delay simulator is an important next check.

The numerical teacher is not an oracle.

Multi-start SLSQP gives best-found local solutions under chosen bounds and λ = 0.01. Those choices define what the model learns. Different initializations, horizons, penalties or actuator constraints can change the labels. Teacher failures are replaced in the main data, so success there can overstate coverage of all sampled plants.

The task currently requires all five empirical features.

Our inputs avoid explicit identification of K, ωn and ζ, but require a sufficiently long, noise-free open-loop experiment. The live physical-input mode derives the same five empirical features before prediction; it does not feed physical parameters directly into the MLP. Missing empirical features are not supported. “Empirical-only tuning” is the supported claim.

Acceptance and accuracy need context.

A 93.73% acceptance rate still leaves 94 main-test misses. ITAE and J ratios are conditional on successful pairs and have different coverage across models. R2 alone cannot establish safe control. The 10% overshoot requirement is a numerical acceptance test, not a guarantee that a learned controller will never exceed it.

A single split leaves uncertainty.

All models share one held-out partition; the final 10K comparison and learning curves do not provide repeated-split confidence intervals. Validation-guided choices reduce leakage but do not remove selection uncertainty. The final settings were locked before test scoring. The extended size study uses validation only and does not alter the frozen final models.
